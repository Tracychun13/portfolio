Newton's Cradle Simulation

An interactive 3D Newton's Cradle built in openFrameworks that lets users pull back the spheres, let go, and watch energy and momentum travel through the cradle in real time. I built it to turn a physics concept that's usually explained on paper into something people can play with.

The problem

Energy conservation and Newton's third law are easy to state but hard to picture. I wanted to make an interactable way to show this for those who don't have the cradle lying around. For my final project in IGME 309 at RIT, I set out to build a simulation that makes those ideas visible and hands-on.

The idea also had to meet a full technical spec. The physics needed a numerical integration method and linear momentum transfer. The geometry had to show scaling, shearing, quaternion rotation with SLERP, and a Bézier curve. It also had to be built using openFrameworks, compile and run in Visual Studio 2022 with nothing extra to install.

How I got there

The process

  1. Step 01

    Planning the experience

    I started by deciding what someone should take away from the simulation: energy is conserved, and every collision has an equal and opposite reaction. From there I planned three core features: a five-sphere cradle users could view from any angle, a way to grab and lift the spheres, and a visual signal at the moment of impact.

  2. Step 02

    Building the 3D scene

    I built the frame as a 3D box and hung five spheres from it, calculating each anchor point from the ball count and spacing so the row always stays centered. Each string is drawn as a line from its anchor to its sphere, and the white wireframe on a black background keeps the motion easy to read. An orbit camera (ofEasyCam) lets users rotate and zoom around the cradle to watch it from any angle.

  3. Step 03

    Physics & collisions

    Every frame, the simulation applies gravity and damping to each sphere's velocity, then updates its position with Euler integration. To make each sphere swing like a pendulum, a constraint pulls it back to the string's length and removes any velocity along the string, leaving only motion along the arc. For collisions, I check the distance between neighboring spheres. When two overlap and are moving toward each other, I apply a mass-weighted impulse along the line between their centers so momentum passes down the row, then push them apart equally so they never sink into each other.

  4. Step 04

    Mouse interaction

    Users right-click a sphere and drag it to pull it back. To keep the sphere on its string while dragging, I convert the mouse position into 3D space at the sphere's depth, clamp the horizontal offset to the string length, and solve for the height with the Pythagorean theorem, so the sphere always traces the correct arc. The selected sphere turns red, and the camera locks during the drag so the view doesn't spin. On release, the sphere gets a push along the arc and swings back into the row.

  5. Step 05

    Controls & polish

    I added an on-screen GUI panel with sliders for gravity and damping so users can experiment and see how each one changes the swing, and an R key that resets the spheres, colors and sliders to their defaults. The result

The final simulation is a fully interactive 3D Newton's Cradle that runs in real time. Users can rotate around it, pull back a sphere, watch momentum move through the row, and change gravity and damping live to see how the motion responds.

  • Simulated pendulum motion in C++ with Euler integration and a string-length constraint.
  • Transferred momentum between spheres with mass-weighted impulse collisions and overlap correction.
  • Built 3D mouse dragging that keeps each sphere on its arc, plus live GUI controls and a keyboard reset.

What I learned

My biggest takeaway was that realistic motion comes as much from constraints as from forces. Gravity alone just pulls a sphere straight down; the string constraint and collision impulses are what make it behave like a real cradle. I also got comfortable moving between screen space and 3D world space, which is what made dragging feel natural. Next, I'd want the simulation to be more interactive and let users change the number of spheres or show the energy values on screen.
